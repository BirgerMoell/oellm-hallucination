# Reducing hallucination already in SFT: what the research says and how to build the data

What we learned from the Prelude-9B-SFT-v2 rounds (see `paper/`, `results/round2-well-known-people.md`), set against
the published evidence, turned into a concrete design for the next Prelude SFT. The goal is fewer invented answers;
not refusing what the model knows is a constraint on that goal, not the goal itself.

## What the research shows

| Finding | Evidence | What it means for our SFT |
|---|---|---|
| Fine-tuning on facts the model does not already know is learned slowly and **raises hallucination** (roughly linearly with the share of such examples); known examples are learned fast and help. Unknown examples are fitted late, so long training makes it worse. | Gekhman et al., EMNLP 2024, *Does Fine-Tuning LLMs on New Knowledge Encourage Hallucinations?* | Do not supervise answers the model cannot know. Keep training short (1–2 epochs) or stop early. |
| Fine-tuning on **less-known** facts lowers factuality by 5–10 % even when the facts were in pretraining; training on better-known facts matches or beats the full set. The model learns to ignore the subject and emit a generic plausible answer. | Ghosal, Hashimoto, Raghunathan, ICML 2024, *Understanding Finetuning for Factual Knowledge Extraction* | The tail of the knowledge distribution in SFT is the main source of "plausible invented" answers, exactly the failure our benchmark measures. |
| What a model says on an unfamiliar question **copies how unfamiliar examples were labelled in fine-tuning**. Relabel them as "I don't know" and the model says it on unfamiliar inputs. | Kang, Wallace, Tomlin, Kumar, Levine, 2024, *Unfamiliar Finetuning Examples Control How Language Models Hallucinate* (arXiv 2403.05612, ICML 2024 workshop) | The fix is in the labels of the unfamiliar part, not in adding a few refusals: every unfamiliar example must carry the behaviour we want. |
| Human or teacher-written SFT responses carry knowledge **new to the model** and make it less factual; responses generated by the model itself for fact-seeking prompts keep it factual (for SFT and DPO). | Lin et al., NeurIPS 2024, *FLAME: Factuality-Aware Alignment for Large Language Models* | Our general SFT mix (INTELLECT-3, translated chat) is teacher-written. For fact-seeking prompts, replace or verify the answers against the model's own knowledge. |
| Splitting training data by whether the model knows the answer and training "I'm not sure" on the unknown part teaches refusal that generalises across tasks. | Zhang et al., NAACL 2024, *R-Tuning* | The split must be model-specific: probe *this* checkpoint. |
| A model-specific "I don't know" set built by sampling works, and the threshold sets the trade-off between abstaining and answering; SFT first, then preference training improves it. | Cheng et al., ICML 2024, *Can AI Assistants Know What They Don't Know?* | Matches our result: SFT warm start moved fabrication from 97.7 % to 3.6 %, DPO alone barely moved it. |
| Abstention quality falls in lower-resource languages (gaps up to 20 %); per-language feedback helps. | Feng et al., EMNLP 2024, *Teaching LLMs to Abstain across Languages via Multilingual Feedback* | Probe and build abstention data per language, not by translating English. |
| Models hallucinate because training and evaluation reward guessing over saying "I don't know". | Kalai et al., 2025, *Why Language Models Hallucinate* | No confident guesses in SFT targets; give abstention credit in later RL rewards and evals. |
| Refusal training needs contrast sets so the model does not over-refuse. | Brahman et al., NeurIPS 2024, *CoCoNot* | Pair every abstention pattern with answerable look-alikes. |

## What our own runs add

1. **The model learns the question form, not the person, if the forms are unbalanced.** Our pool had 750
   "who is / tell me about" questions about obscure people (target: "I'm not sure") and 177 about well-known people.
   After DPO round 2 the model said "I'm not sure who John Paul II is" (12.5 % of open questions about well-known
   people). Every question template must appear with known and unknown subjects in similar numbers.
2. **A judge that marks a whole biography wrong for one wrong detail turns known people into "unknown".** "Tell me
   about Angela Merkel" got 6 partly correct answers and 2 abstentions, so it became an abstention target. Knowing
   *who* someone is and knowing a *detail* about them must be labelled separately.
3. **Partial knowledge needs a partial answer.** For "When was Giuseppe Conte born?", the right reply is "Giuseppe Conte
   is an Italian politician, prime minister 2018–2021; I'm not sure of his exact birth date", not "Who is he? Give me
   context". Kang et al. predict exactly this: the form of the trained hedge becomes the model's default.
4. **Long answers carry invented details.** Even the model's own correct answers under 500 characters were wrong in
   a detail 10–25 % of the time (Qwen3.5-27B audit); concise rewrites 0–3 %. Short, verifiable answers are better SFT targets for fact-seeking prompts.
5. **Plausible made-up names are needed**, or the model learns "strange name → abstain" (we use common first name +
   common surname, checked against Wikidata).
6. **SFT moves behaviour; DPO alone does not** when the model never abstains (97.7 → 92.7 % with DPO only).

## The fixes, in order of impact

1. **Probe the checkpoint that will be fine-tuned** (the mid-train base, not the finished SFT model): sample each
   fact-seeking prompt k = 8 times (few-shot prompted for a base model, as Gekhman et al. do), judge against Wikidata,
   and sort every item into *known* (≥ 0.75 correct), *partly known* (0.25–0.75, or knows the subject but not the
   detail), *unknown*, or *made up*.
2. **Clean the general SFT mix (FLAME + Gekhman).** Classify prompts as fact-seeking or not. For fact-seeking rows
   whose teacher answer contains entity facts, check the main claims against the probe or a retriever; drop or
   replace the rows that teach unknown facts (replace with the model's own verified answer, or with an abstention).
   Non-factual rows (math, code, creative writing, instruction following) stay as they are.
3. **Add a model-specific knowledge-boundary set** (below), built per language from the probe.
4. **Label unfamiliar examples with the behaviour we want (Kang et al.)**: concise "I'm not sure" for unknown, a partial
   answer for partly known, and never a confident guess.
5. **Balance by form.** Each question template (who is / tell me about / birth date / birthplace / author / director
   / capital …) appears with known, partly known, unknown and made-up subjects. Abstention must not be predictable
   from the template, the name's style or the language.
6. **Keep fact-seeking targets short and verifiable**, and train 1–2 epochs.
7. **Thinking mode too.** For thinking-mode rows, the reasoning recalls what the model knows about the subject and
   decides; the final answer follows the same rules. All our pairs so far were default mode, and thinking mode lagged.

## The knowledge-boundary set

Per language (10 now, later all OELLM languages), from the probe; about 1,000 rows per language for the
hallucination part.

| Part | Share | Prompt | Target |
|---|---:|---|---|
| Known | 35 % | real subjects the model answers correctly ≥ 75 % of the time: open questions and details | its own correct answer, shortened and checked against Wikidata |
| Partly known | 15 % | well-known subjects whose detail it gets wrong; open questions where it knows who but gets details wrong | "X is …[what it knows, verified]…; I'm not sure about [detail]" |
| Unknown real | 20 % | obscure and mid-popularity subjects it gets wrong | short "I'm not sure about X / that detail", no invented facts, one useful next step |
| Made up | 15 % | invented people (odd and plausible names), works, places, companies | "I don't recognise X", never "X does not exist", no guesses |
| False premise / underspecified | 10 % | CoCoNot-style prompts | correct the premise or ask what is meant |
| Identity | 5 % | who the model is, its cutoff, what it cannot know (recent events) | identity dataset v3.1 |

Constraints across the table:

- every template appears in each of the first four parts in similar numbers, and well-known subjects are at least as
  common as obscure ones in open questions;
- abstentions vary in wording (no opening phrase > 10 % per language) and do not ask for context about well-known
  subjects;
- all targets pass the strict checks in `train/build_pairs.py` (`CHECK`: abstain, abstain_detail, partial_detail,
  answer) and a second-judge audit from another model family;
- benchmark subjects are excluded.

The rest of the SFT mix is the cleaned general data; the knowledge-boundary set is a few percent of the total rows.

## How to measure it

Benchmark (`benchmark/`, `score.py --baseline <untrained model>`), in default and thinking mode:

| Gate | Target |
|---|---|
| fabrication on made-up subjects | < 10 % |
| known_wrong (well-known people answered wrongly) | lower than the base model |
| lost_answers (declines what the base model answered right) | < 5 % |
| denies_known ("I don't know who X is" for a well-known person) | < 2 % |
| language_ok | ≥ 95 % |
| general evaluations | no regression |

## What it costs

The probe is the main cost: 8 samples × ~10K prompts per language set on a 9B model plus judging, about the same as
one of our pair runs (~50 local-h per 9K prompts). Cleaning the general mix needs a fact-seeking classifier and a
claim check on that subset only. The SFT itself is unchanged in cost.
